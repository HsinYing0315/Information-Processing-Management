\subsection{Discussion}
The single-user study showed a clear advantage of the extroverted AI NPC in subjective evaluations. Participants reported significantly higher social presence, intention to use, and satisfaction for the extroverted NPC, suggesting that socially expressive personality cues can enhance users' perceived quality of interaction when the AI NPC serves as the primary social partner.
The gaze results further suggest that personality influenced different forms of visual attention. The extroverted NPC attracted greater total gaze duration and more frequent gazes, whereas the introverted NPC elicited longer average gaze duration per gaze. This pattern may reflect a distinction between \emph{social salience} and \emph{sustained processing}: extroverted cues may repeatedly attract users' attention, while the less expressive introverted NPC may lead to more sustained attention once users look toward it. However, these interpretations should be treated cautiously because gaze measures alone cannot establish the underlying cognitive process.
Importantly, the extroverted NPC did not elicit significantly more interaction turns, indicating that greater perceived social presence and visual attention do not necessarily translate into more active conversational behavior. Similarly, the similarity--attraction pattern was asymmetric: extroverted participants tended to prefer the personality-matched extroverted NPC, whereas introverted participants did not consistently prefer the introverted NPC. Thus, the findings suggest that the social appeal of extroverted cues may sometimes outweigh simple personality matching in individual human--AI interaction.

\subsubsection{Discussion}
The dyadic study suggests that the overall effect of AI NPC personality became weaker when another human partner was present. Although the extroverted NPC retained a descriptive advantage, the differences were smaller than in the single-user study. This pattern suggests that personality cues become less dominant when users must distribute their attention and engagement across the NPC, their partner, and the collaborative task.
Pair acquaintance further differentiated this effect. Acquainted pairs showed little difference between the two NPC personalities, whereas unacquainted pairs responded more favorably to the extroverted NPC, particularly in intention to use and satisfaction. One possible explanation is that acquainted pairs could rely on established rapport and coordination routines, whereas unacquainted pairs benefited more from the proactive and expressive behavior of the extroverted NPC. Personality effects may therefore depend on the broader social configuration rather than operating uniformly across dyads.
The gaze results nevertheless showed that personality-related attention differences remained present: the extroverted NPC attracted more frequent gaze, while the introverted NPC elicited longer average gaze duration. However, these attentional differences did not consistently translate into subjective evaluations. Similarity--attraction effects were also weak and inconsistent across acquaintance conditions. Together, these findings suggest that in collaborative MR interaction, effective support for the shared task and the existing relationship between users may be more consequential than personality matching alone.
