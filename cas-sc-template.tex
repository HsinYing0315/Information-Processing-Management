\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}

This study investigates how the effects of AI NPC personality extend from individual interaction to dyadic collaboration, and how NPC personality and pair acquaintance jointly influence users’ perceptions of the AI, reliance on the AI, and collaborative engagement in dyadic settings.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{RQ1.} How does AI NPC personality influence users’ perceptions and interaction behaviors in an individual MR museum experience?
    \item \textbf{RQ2.} To what extent does personality similarity between users and AI NPCs shape users’ evaluations of the NPC?
    \item \textbf{RQ3.} How does pair acquaintance shape users’ reliance on AI NPCs in a collaborative MR experience?
    \item \textbf{RQ4.} To what extent does AI NPC personality influence collaborative engagement in dyadic interaction?
    \item \textbf{RQ5.} Does pair acquaintance moderate the effect of AI NPC personality on collaborative engagement?
\end{itemize}

\section{Lab Experiment: Single User}\label{sec:lab}

This study uses a within-subjects MR tour experiment to examine the effects of introverted and extroverted NPCs on multiple dependent variables, enabling a comparative evaluation while minimizing individual differences.

Accordingly, this study addressed the following research questions: 
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{RQ1.} How does AI NPC personality influence users' perceptions and interaction behaviors in an individual MR museum experience?
    \item \textbf{RQ2.} To what extent does personality similarity between users and AI NPCs shape users' evaluations of the NPC?
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Participants}

Participants’ extraversion was assessed using the Extraversion domain of the BFI-2 questionnaire. The scale consists of 12 items rated on a five-point Likert scale, yielding a possible summed score ranging from 12 to 60. A total score of 36 represents the theoretical midpoint of the scale, corresponding to an average item response of 3. Based on this midpoint, participants scoring 36 or below were classified as introverts, whereas those scoring above 36 were classified as extroverts. A total of 16 participants aged between 20 and 30 years were recruited, comprising eight participants in each group.

\subsection{Experimental Design and Procedure}

The study adopted a within-subject design in which each participant interacted with both an introverted and an extroverted AI NPC. To control for potential order effects, participants were randomly assigned to one of two interaction orders.

Before the experiment, participants received an overview of the study procedure and reviewed several example questions that could be asked during the interaction. They then wore a Microsoft HoloLens 2 MR headset and interacted with each AI NPC for up to eight minutes. Each interaction began with a self-introduction by the NPC, followed by two predefined questions designed to initiate the conversation.

After completing both interaction sessions, participants undertook a knowledge task to assess whether they had sufficiently engaged with the exhibit content. Participants who scored below 80\% were excluded from the analysis. They subsequently completed a post-experiment questionnaire and participated in a short semi-structured interview. The entire study lasted approximately 35--40 minutes.

\subsection{Measures}
\subsubsection{Subjective Measures}

Participants' perceptions of the two AI NPCs were assessed using post-interaction questionnaires administered on a five-point Likert scale. The measured constructs included social presence (SP), continuance intention to use (ITU), and satisfaction (SAT).

Social presence was measured using five items adapted from Gefen and Straub~\cite{GefenStraub2004}. The original scale demonstrated satisfactory reliability and convergent validity, with a composite reliability of $\mathrm{CR}=0.919$ and $\sqrt{\mathrm{AVE}}=0.836$. Continuance intention to use was measured using items adapted from Li and Wang~\cite{LiWang2023} ($\alpha=0.93$, $\mathrm{CR}=0.94$, and $\mathrm{AVE}=0.78$). Satisfaction was assessed using items adapted from Shi et al.~\cite{Shietal2023} ($\mathrm{CR}=0.910$ and $\mathrm{AVE}=0.717$).

\subsubsection{Behavioral Measures}

In addition to the subjective measures, behavioral data were collected through system logs, direct observation, and eye-tracking recordings from the MR headset. These measures were included to examine whether the personalities of the AI NPCs influenced participants' observable interaction and visual-attention patterns.

Interaction frequency was operationalized as the number of participant--NPC interactions, including both verbal questions and selections of predefined prompts. Visual attention was measured using fixation counts and fixation durations across three predefined Areas of Interest (AOIs): the AI NPC, the physical exhibit, and the virtual exhibit. These data were used to calculate the total gaze duration and the distribution of visual attention across the three AOIs.

\subsection{Result}

To examine how AI NPC personality affected participants' experiences in the MR tour, both subjective questionnaire responses and objective behavioral data were analyzed. The subjective analysis focused on participants' evaluations of the introverted and extroverted
Al NPCs across social presence (SP), intention to use (ITU), and satisfaction (SAT). The objective analysis examined interaction logs and eye-tracking measures, including the number of questions asked, gaze duration, fixation counts, and average fixation duration. In addition, participant personality was considered to explore whether personality similarity influenced subjective evaluations and visual attention patterns.

\subsubsection{Subjective Data Result}

To address RQ1, we first examined whether NPC personality influenced participants' subjective evaluations. Shapiro–Wilk tests were conducted on the within-participant difference scores between the extroverted and introverted NPC conditions. The difference scores for SP did not significantly deviate from normality (p = .0942), whereas those for ITU, and SAT violated the normality assumption (p < .05). Given the small sample size (N = 16) and the non-normal distributions observed for several constructs, Wilcoxon signed-rank tests were used consistently for all within-subject comparisons. Because tied difference scores were present in more than 10\% of the observations, sign tests and paired permutation tests were additionally conducted as robustness checks. All three methods yielded consistent results for SP, showing significantly higher social presence ratings for the extroverted than the introverted AI NPC (p < .001).

\subsubsection{Objective Data Result}

To further address RQ1, we examined whether NPC personality was reflected in participants' interaction frequency and visual-attention behavior. Analysis of participants’ interaction logs showed that participants asked an average of 3.8 questions to the introverted AI (SD = 1.34) and 4.4 questions to the extroverted AI (SD = 1.25) (Figure 8 and Table 4). However, the difference in the number of questions asked between the two AI NPC personalities was not statistically significant ($p > .05$).

In addition, gaze analysis showed that the AI NPCs received the highest proportion of gaze duration and fixation counts, followed by the virtual and physical exhibits. Wilcoxon signed-rank tests further indicated that participants exhibited significantly greater total gaze duration ($p = .018$) and fixation counts ($p = .017$) toward the extroverted AI than toward the introverted AI. In contrast, the introverted AI elicited significantly longer average fixation durations (Introvert: 6.97 s; Extrovert: 5.82 s; $p = .025$), as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:gaze-duration}, suggesting that individual fixations on the introverted AI tended to be more sustained. Taken together, these findings provide partial evidence that the extroverted AI attracted greater overall visual attention, as reflected by longer total gaze duration and more frequent fixations, while individual fixations were longer for the introverted AI.

\subsubsection{Similarity Attraction}

To address RQ2 and assess whether personality similarity influenced participants’ evaluations of the AI NPCs, Mann–Whitney U tests were conducted across participant personality groups. Extroverted participants consistently showed positive similarity scores, indicating a preference for the personality-matched extroverted AI across social presence, intention to use, and satisfaction. In contrast, introverted participants showed negative similarity scores, suggesting either a preference for the dissimilar extroverted AI or no clear similarity-based preference. The preference for the extroverted AI was significantly stronger among extroverted participants than among introverted participants across all constructs ($p < .05$; Table~\ref{tab:attraction_test}). These findings therefore provide partial evidence for a similarity--attraction pattern, with the similarity–attraction effect emerging primarily among extroverted participants.

Consistent with these subjective evaluations, the eye-tracking results provide behavioral, yet conditional, support for the similarity–attraction hypothesis. Extroverted participants showed positive mean differences in gaze count (+35.88) and gaze duration (+11.86 ms), indicating greater visual attention toward the personality-matched extroverted AI. In contrast, introverted participants exhibited negative differences, directing more visual attention toward the personality-dissimilar extroverted AI. Mann–Whitney U tests further revealed significant between-group differences in both gaze duration ($U = 11.0$, $p < .05$, $r = .55$) and gaze count ($U = 9.0$, $p < .05$, $r = .60$). Taken together, these findings suggest that participant personality significantly shaped gaze allocation, while the similarity–attraction effect was observed more consistently among extroverted than introverted participants.

\subsection{Discussioin}

This study showed that AI NPC personality meaningfully shaped users' experiences in individual MR interaction, but its effects were not consistent across all outcome measures. The extroverted NPC elicited significantly higher perceived social presence and intention to use, and also attracted greater overall visual attention in terms of total gaze duration and gaze count. In contrast, the introverted NPC elicited longer average gaze durations, suggesting a different pattern of sustained visual attention. Together, these findings indicate that socially expressive personality cues can enhance users' subjective perceptions of an AI NPC, while their behavioral effects are more selective. The similarity--attraction analysis further suggested that extroverted participants tended to respond more favorably to the extroverted NPC, providing partial support for the role of user--agent personality similarity.

These findings have two implications for understanding personality in human-Al interaction. First, perceived social presence, visual attention, and behavioral engagement appear to reflect different aspects of users' responses to Al NPCS, rather than a single unified effect. An NPC that is perceived as more socially present may attract more attention without necessarily eliciting more active interaction. Second, the effects of Al personality appear to depend not only on the agent's expressed traits but also on the characteristics of the user, suggesting that personality effects are relational rather than purely agent-driven.

Importantly, this study examined users interacting with the Al NPCs individually, where the NPC served as the primary social interaction partner. In most museum experiences, however, users must also attend to and coordinate with another person. The presence of a partner may therefore alter how attention, reliance, and social engagement are distributed between the Al and human interaction partners. This motivated the subsequent dyadic studies, which examined whether personality effects observed at the individual level would persist, weaken, or take different forms when the Al NPC became part of a broader human-human-Al interaction.

\section{Pair}\label{sec:pair}

Building on the individual user study, the next stage examined whether the effects of AI NPC personality would persist or change when the interaction became socially shared. In many museum visits, users experience exhibits with companions rather than alone, which may redistribute attention and engagement between the AI NPC and the human partner. To address this context, we conducted a dyadic pilot study as an intermediate step toward a formal pair study. The pilot study explored how two co-located users interacted with each other and with the AI NPC in the MR museum, and helped identify interaction patterns, system issues, and study-design considerations for the subsequent formal study.

\subsection{Pilot Study}

The pilot study served as an exploratory bridge between the individual MR study and the formal dyadic study. Whereas the individual study examined one-to-one interaction between a user and an AI NPC, the pilot introduced a human partner into the MR museum experience to explore personality-driven Al NPCs in a shared interaction setting. It examined whether exhibit-specific AI NPCs could support dyadic interaction, how pairs coordinated their attention and communication during the shared experience, and what usability, personality manipulation, and task design issues should be refined before the formal study.

\subsubsection{System and Study Design}

The pilot study was conducted at the Yang Ming Oceanic Culture \& Art Museum in Keelung, Taiwan. Three acquainted pairs experienced a co-located MR museum environment using Microsoft HoloLens 2. Within the environment, participants could interact with physical artifacts, virtual exhibit content, and two exhibit-specific AI NPC guides.

\subsubsection{Personality Design}

AI NPC personality was operationalized as the primary within-pair independent variable. Drawing on the Big Five personality framework, we focused on extraversion as the target dimension for personality design, following prior work on personality perception in conversational agents. The manipulation was implemented through both language and speech design. At the language level, GPT-4 prompts were used to specify each NPC’s conversational style, degree of proactivity, and emotional expressiveness. At the speech level, Azure TTS parameters were adjusted to further differentiate the perceived interaction style of the two NPCs.

In the introverted condition, the NPC was designed to provide more concise responses, adopt a reserved conversational style, and express lower emotional intensity. In the extroverted condition, the NPC was designed to use more enthusiastic language, initiate interaction more proactively, and display higher emotional expressiveness. Although each NPC was also embedded within an exhibit-specific historical identity, the core manipulation centered on contrasting introverted and extroverted interaction styles.

\subsubsection{Collaborative Design}

Unlike the individual study, which used predefined questions to scaffold user-Al NPC interaction, the dyadic pilot study used an asymmetric clue-based task to elicit both human-human communication and human-Al NPC engagement. The task was structured around exhibit-related clues distributed asymmetrically between the two participants. Each participant received the same background story and task questions but was provided with different exhibit-related clues. This asymmetric information structure required participants to exchange information, discuss their interpretations, and coordinate their next steps in order to progress through the task.

To support this collaborative structure, the NPCs' task-related behavior was further constrained through system prompts. The NPCs were instructed to recognize the ongoing collaborative task, avoid directly revealing the answer, and provide indirect hints instead. They were also prompted to treat the interaction as a multi-user conversation and to consider the shared conversation history when responding.

\subsubsection{Participants and Procedure}

The pilot study involved six participants forming three acquainted pairs. Five participants were male and one was female. Participants were between 23 and 24 years old ($M=23.5$, $SD=0.5$). Based on self-reported trait extraversion, two participants were categorized as introverted and four as extroverted. Given the small sample size, these individual differences were treated descriptively rather than used for inferential comparison.

Each pair completed the collaborative MR task while interacting with both AI NPCs. Questionnaire responses, system interaction logs, gaze data, and observational notes were collected to assess the feasibility of the dyadic interaction design and identify issues requiring refinement.

\subsubsection{Pilot Findings and Design Issues}

\paragraph{Personality Manipulation}

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.5\linewidth]{figs/pilot_manipulation.png}

To assess whether the intended personality contrast was perceived by participants, we calculated a net perceived extraversion score by subtracting perceived introversion from perceived extraversion ($\mathrm{PE}-\mathrm{PI}$). Higher values indicated stronger perceived extraversion.

As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:pilot_manipulation}, the observed pattern did not align with the intended personality manipulation. The NPC designed as extroverted showed a lower mean net extraversion score than the NPC designed as introverted. This mismatch suggested that the initial personality cues were not sufficiently robust in the dyadic MR setting and motivated further refinement before the formal study.

\paragraph{Task Difficulty}

Participants reported a low-to-moderate level of perceived task difficulty ($M=3.08$, $SD=0.66$, range $=2$--$4$ on a 7-point scale). The task was generally manageable while still requiring participants to interpret clues and coordinate with their partners.

\paragraph{Interaction and Usability Issues}

Observations during the pilot revealed several interaction issues. Some participants had difficulty reliably activating the virtual speech-input button, which occasionally disrupted attempts to initiate conversation with the NPC. Participants also experienced uncertainty regarding whether the system was available, listening, processing, or responding.

The NPCs' task-related guidance was also sometimes too indirect. Because the initial prompts strongly constrained the NPCs from revealing task answers, some hints provided insufficient scaffolding for participants to progress efficiently through the task.

Based on these design issues, we refined both the system design and the study design before conducting the subsequent experiment. First, we expanded participant recruitment to include both acquainted and unacquainted pairs, allowing us to examine how prior acquaintance may shape collaborative interaction with AI NPCs.

Second, we improved the speech-input interface by making system states more explicit. The interface was revised to help participants distinguish between available to speak, listening, processing, and responding states, thereby reducing uncertainty during turn-taking with the AI NPCs.

Third, we refined the personality prompts to strengthen the distinction between the introverted and extroverted NPCs. Specifically, the personality-related instructions were placed on the top in the prompt structure and marked as important to increase their priority during response generation. This refinement was intended to improve the consistency between the system-defined personality and participants’ perceived personality.
Finally, we adjusted the task-related prompting strategy to reduce task difficulty while preserving the collaborative structure. 

Instead of only providing indirect hints, the NPCs were allowed to offer more scaffolded guidance, such as presenting possible options or narrowing the range of interpretations. This refinement was intended to help participants progress through the task without allowing the NPCs to simply provide the correct answers.

\subsection{Experiment}

Building on the findings and design iterations from the pilot study, Study 3 conducted a formal evaluation of the dyadic MR experience with a larger sample of acquainted and unacquainted pairs. The study examined how NPC personality and pair acquaintance shaped users’ perceptions of the AI, reliance on the NPCs, and collaborative engagement.

Accordingly, Study 3 addressed the following research questions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{RQ3.} How does pair familiarity shape users' reliance on AI NPCs in a collaborative MR experience?
    \item \textbf{RQ4.} To what extent does AI NPC personality influence collaborative engagement in dyadic interaction?
    \item \textbf{RQ5.} Does pair familiarity moderate the effect of AI NPC personality on collaborative engagement?
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Participants and Procedure}

\subsubsection{Experimental Design}

\subsubsection{Manipulation Check}

Before addressing the main research questions, we examined whether the two AI NPCs were perceived as having the intended personality traits. Four planned comparisons were conducted using participants' perceived introversion (PI) and perceived extraversion (PE) ratings.

For the introverted NPC, perceived introversion was higher than perceived extraversion ($M_{\mathrm{PI}} = 4.46$ vs. $M_{\mathrm{PE}} = 3.61$), although this difference did not reach statistical significance (Wilcoxon $p = .143$). In contrast, the extroverted NPC was rated significantly higher in extraversion than introversion ($M_{\mathrm{PE}} = 4.82$ vs. $M_{\mathrm{PI}} = 3.36$, $p = .013$). Cross-NPC comparisons further showed that the extroverted NPC was perceived as significantly more extraverted than the introverted NPC ($4.82$ vs. $3.61$, $p = .033$), whereas the introverted NPC was perceived as significantly more introverted than the extroverted NPC ($4.46$ vs. $3.36$, $p = .050$). As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:pair_manipulation}, net extraversion scores ($\mathrm{PE}-\mathrm{PI}$) were generally negative for the introverted NPC and positive for the extroverted NPC.

Overall, three of the four comparisons were statistically significant and all four were in the intended direction, indicating that participants generally perceived the personality manipulation as intended. However, the distinction between introversion and extraversion within the introverted NPC was comparatively weaker.

To further examine whether pair acquaintance shaped responses to NPC personality, the dyadic sample was divided into acquainted and unacquainted pairs ($n=14$ participants in each group). As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:contructs_vs}, acquainted pairs showed little differentiation between the introverted and extroverted NPCs across social presence (SP), intention to use (ITU), and satisfaction (SAT). Mean difference scores were small across all three constructs, suggesting that acquainted pairs evaluated the two NPC personalities similarly (SP: $M_{\mathrm{diff}} =0.13$; ITU: $M_{\mathrm{diff}}=0.10$; SAT: $M_{\mathrm{diff}}=0.36$). Wilcoxon signed-rank tests (Table~\ref{tab:acquainted_construct_results}) indicated no significant differences for any construct (SP: $p=.649$; ITU: $p=.965$; SAT: $p=.875$), with correspondingly small effect sizes.

In contrast, unacquainted pairs showed a more consistent pattern of favorable evaluations toward the extroverted NPC. Positive mean differences were observed for SP ($M_{\mathrm{diff}}=0.50$), ITU ($M_{\mathrm{diff}}=0.62$), and SAT ($M_{\mathrm{diff}}=0.60$), indicating higher average ratings for the extroverted NPC across all three constructs. Wilcoxon signed-rank tests (Table~\ref{tab:unacquainted_construct_results}) indicated significant differences for ITU ($p=.041$, $r=.55$) and SAT ($p=.033$, $r=.57$), while SP showed only a marginal trend ($p=.050$, $r=.52$). Permutation tests showed a similar pattern, with significant differences for ITU ($p=.046$) and SAT ($p=.044$), whereas SP did not reach significance ($p=.096$). Taken together, these results suggest that the favorable evaluations of the extroverted NPC were more pronounced among unacquainted pairs, particularly for intention to use and satisfaction, while the evidence for social presence was less robust.

Because participants did not always perceive the NPC personalities consistently with the experimentally assigned conditions, the similarity--attraction analysis was based on each participant's perceived NPC personality rather than the predefined introverted and extroverted labels. For each outcome, a similarity score was calculated as the rating for the NPC perceived as more similar to the participant's own personality minus the rating for the NPC perceived as more dissimilar. For acquainted pairs, Table~\ref{tab:similarity_acquainted} indicates that similarity effects differed according to participants' own personality. Extroverted participants generally showed positive similarity scores, indicating more favorable evaluations of personality-similar NPCs, whereas introverted participants tended to show negative scores. Between-group comparisons indicated a significant difference only for satisfaction (SAT; $U=4.5$, $p=.014$, $r=.68$), with extroverted participants reporting greater satisfaction with personality-similar NPCs. Differences for social presence (SP; $U=12.0$, $p=.136$, $r=.42$) and intention to use (ITU; $U=9.0$, $p=.060$, $r=.52$) did not reach statistical significance, although the latter showed a similar directional pattern. Thus, the subjective results provided only partial evidence for similarity--attraction among acquainted pairs.

Although mean similarity scores were generally positive, the differences between introverted and extroverted participants were not statistically reliable, providing insufficient evidence that perceived personality similarity systematically increased evaluations of the NPC in unacquainted-pair interactions.

\subsubsection{Discussion}

Personality effect show stronger between unacquainted pairs may reflect differences in how acquainted and unacquainted pairs engaged with the NPC during the shared experience. Acquainted pairs may have relied more on their existing rapport and coordination habits, making the NPC's personality less central to their evaluation. By contrast, unacquainted pairs may have benefited more from the extroverted PC's proactive and expressive interaction style, which could have made the collaborative experience feel easier and more engaging. Rather than indicating a uniform advantage of extroversion, this pattern suggests that the value of NPC personality cues may depend on the social context of the dyad.

The similarity--attraction effect was limited and context-dependent. Among acquainted pairs, extroverted participants showed some preference for personality-similar NPCs, particularly in satisfaction, whereas introverted participants did not exhibit the same pattern. No reliable similarity effect was observed among unacquainted pairs. Moreover, subjective preference was not reflected in gaze behavior, suggesting that personality similarity may influence evaluation without necessarily altering visual attention.

\subsection{Cross-Study Analysis}

To further comparing the collected data between single user and pair users, we seperate them into three group: single/acquainted pair/unacquainted pair. And we compared their Social Presence(SP), Intention to Use(ITU), and Satisfaction(SAT).

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\linewidth]{figs/output.png}
    \caption{Boxplots of Average User Ratings for Introverted vs. Extroverted AI NPCs on SP, ITU, and SAT Constructs}
    \label{fig:boxplots}
\end{figure}

From Figure~\ref{fig:boxplots}, we can tell that overall, participants in the Single User condition exhibited a more pronounced advantage for the extroverted NPC in terms of social presence (SP) and intention to use (ITU). In contrast, the differences observed in the Acquainted Pair and Unacquainted Pair conditions were smaller and less consistent. This pattern suggests that when users interact with an NPC alongside another participant rather than individually, the influence of NPC personality on subjective evaluations may be attenuated by the presence of a human partner.

Because the Single User study employed a 5-point Likert scale, whereas the pair-based studies employed a 7-point Likert scale, all questionnaire responses were normalized to a common 0–1 scale for the subsequent analyses.

To further examine whether the effects differed statistically across the three interaction conditions, a difference score was calculated for each participant and construct by subtracting the rating of the introverted NPC from that of the extroverted NPC. A positive score therefore indicated a relative preference for the extroverted NPC, whereas a negative score indicated a relative preference for the introverted NPC.

Kruskal–Wallis tests were then conducted to compare the distributions of the difference scores across the Single User, Acquainted Pair, and Unacquainted Pair conditions. The results revealed a significant between-condition difference in the SP difference scores, indicating that the relative effect of the extroverted NPC, compared with the introverted NPC, on perceived social presence varied across interaction conditions.

By contrast, the Kruskal–Wallis tests for ITU and satisfaction (SAT) were not statistically significant. Thus, there was insufficient evidence to conclude that the relative advantage of the extroverted NPC in terms of intention to use or satisfaction differed across the three interaction conditions.
